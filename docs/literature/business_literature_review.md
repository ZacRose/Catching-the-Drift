## Literature review

### 1. Hutchings et al. (2009) - The Benguela Current: An ecosystem of four components

https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0079661109001104

**Research focus:** The study provides a multidisciplinary overview of the Benguela Current system
and examines its physical, biological, and ecological characteristics. It describes the Benguela as
one of the world's four major eastern boundary upwelling systems and examines the different
components of the system, including the Angolan, northern Benguela, southern Benguela, and Agulhas
Bank regions.

**Data and methodology:** Rather than conducting a single new modelling or observational
experiment, the authors synthesise existing oceanographic, biological and fisheries research to
describe the Benguela system. The study considers physical processes such as wind-driven upwelling,
ocean circulation and boundary fronts, together with biological productivity, fish populations and
fisheries. It also considers variability across different spatial and temporal scales and the
influence of larger-scale ocean and atmospheric forcing.

**Key findings:** The study shows that the Benguela system is a complex and highly interconnected
ecosystem. Strong wind-driven upwelling contributes to high biological productivity, while
oceanographic features such as fronts, offshore transport, and changes in water properties
influence the distribution and productivity of marine organisms. The authors also highlight
substantial spatial and temporal variability across the Benguela system, as well as the importance
of basin-scale ocean and atmospheric processes. The study discusses the consequences of intensive
resource use and changes in fish stocks, demonstrating the broader ecological and fisheries
significance of the region.

**Relevance to our project:** This study provides important background for selecting the Benguela
Current and the surrounding South Atlantic as our study region. It shows that the Benguela system
is influenced by both regional circulation and larger-scale oceanographic processes, making
transport and connectivity an important aspect of understanding the region. This provides a basis
for our project to investigate drifter trajectories within the Benguela–South Atlantic box and
examine where these drifters eventually end up.

### 2. Drouin & Lozier (2019) - The Surface Pathways of the South Atlantic

https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2019JC015267

**Research focus:** The study investigates the surface pathways of the South Atlantic and examines
the origins and pathways of water entering the Benguela Current. In particular, it revisits the
traditional "cold" and "warm" water routes connecting the South Atlantic with the Pacific and
Indian Oceans.

**Data and methodology:** The authors use observational data, including surface drifter
trajectories from the Global Drifter Program, together with OSCAR surface-current data and
subsurface float observations. Lagrangian trajectory analysis is used to examine how water moves
through the South Atlantic and to identify the pathways connecting different regions.

**Key findings:** The study shows that the Benguela Current receives water from multiple pathways,
including the South Atlantic interior and routes associated with the Pacific and Indian Oceans. The
results demonstrate that surface transport in the South Atlantic is complex, with different
pathways interacting and contributing to connectivity across the basin.

**Relevance to our project:** This study is particularly relevant to our project because it
demonstrates how observational drifter trajectories can be used to investigate surface transport
and connectivity in the South Atlantic. While Drouin and Lozier focus primarily on where water
entering the Benguela Current comes from, our project takes a complementary perspective by
investigating where drifters originating within our selected Benguela Current–South Atlantic region
eventually end up.

## Case review

### Benguela Current Convention - Ecosystem Services

https://www.benguelacc.org/ecosystem-advisory/

The Benguela Current region supports significant maritime and shipping activities as part of its
wider ecosystem services.

- Shipping is one of the major economic activities identified by the Benguela Current Convention,
  alongside oil and gas, diamond mining, fisheries and aquaculture.
- These activities depend on and interact with the marine environment, making an understanding of
  ocean conditions and transport within the region relevant to maritime activities.
- Our project investigates the movement of surface drifters from the Benguela–South Atlantic region
  and identifies where they eventually end up.
- Understanding these observed transport pathways can provide information about the connectivity
  between different parts of the South Atlantic, which is relevant when considering marine
  activities that operate across this interconnected region.

## Summary

**Why this region matters**
- The Benguela Current is a major and highly interconnected ecosystem within the South Atlantic.
- Its dynamics are influenced by both regional oceanographic processes and larger-scale processes
  across the South Atlantic.
- The region is also important for fisheries and maritime activities.
- Studying the Benguela Current together with the surrounding South Atlantic allows us to
  investigate connections between different parts of the basin.

**Why transport/connectivity matters**
- Previous research shows that surface pathways in the South Atlantic are complex and connect
  different regions of the basin.
- Drifter trajectories can be used to investigate these surface transport pathways and
  connectivity.
- Understanding surface transport can provide information about how the Benguela Current is
  connected to other parts of the South Atlantic.

**Why "where it ends up" is a meaningful question**
- The movement of floating material can have practical implications for marine pollution and
  maritime activities.
- Maritime accidents and marine pollution can affect coastal ecosystems, fisheries and tourism in
  the Benguela region.
- Tracking where drifters eventually end up allows us to examine observed surface transport
  pathways from our study region.
- This can help identify which areas are connected to the Benguela–South Atlantic region through
  surface transport.

**2. Drouin & Lozier (2019): two corrections, one addition.**
- The abstract names two datasets: GDP drifter trajectories and the OSCAR surface-current
  product. It doesn't mention subsurface floats; drop them unless the full text has them.
- "Including the South Atlantic interior" isn't in the abstract. The actual headline is about the
  two routes: backward trajectories suggest a sizeable share of Benguela water comes from the
  Pacific via Drake Passage (the "cold" route), only a small part of the Agulhas water leaks into
  the South Atlantic (the "warm" route), and the two datasets disagree on how big the Drake
  Passage share is. Don't quote percentages until someone reads the paper.
- Add: it uses the same GDP dataset we do, which makes it the closest precedent for our method.

**3. BCC web page: right list, wrong emphasis.** The page has sections on oil and gas, diamond
mining, fisheries, shipping and aquaculture (plus tourism under sport fishing), and the parties
are Angola, Namibia and South Africa. It doesn't call shipping "a major economic activity"; it
says ports "play a crucial role in the economy of the region". More useful for us: it says major
international shipping routes cross the region and that this is a pollution risk. That links
directly to "where does floating material go". It's a web page, not peer-reviewed, so cite it
that way.

**4. Novelli et al. (2020): add an entry and a caveat.** It's cited in the background draft but has
no entry above. Accurate as far as it goes. In a wave tank, drogued drifters didn't follow oil,
while undrogued drifters (and thin bamboo plates) spread at the same rate and direction as crude
oil slicks. In the field, about 25% of undrogued drifters reached the coast against about 5% of
drogued ones, mostly where Deepwater Horizon oil landed. Caveat: these were probably Gulf of
Mexico research drifters (CARTHE), not the GDP buoys we use, so the result may not carry over
directly (project_plan.txt decision 6a).

**5. Scope: "where drifters eventually end up".** Our P only follows drifters inside the box.
Leaving the box is one state, "left R", and we don't track where they go after that (decision
11b). So the README can promise: where material from a cell goes within the Benguela–South
Atlantic box, and how much of it leaves. It can't promise destinations outside the box.
