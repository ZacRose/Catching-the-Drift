1. Recommended project direction: "Where things end up"
This maps directly onto starting project 3.2.2 in the GDP overview document: given something entering the water at a point in region R, where has the surface flow taken it a week, a month, a year later? The deliverable is a surface-transport operator (a transition matrix or equivalent) that another analyst could load and iterate forward.
Justification:
It naturally decomposes into three layers that build on each other rather than sitting side by side: an engineering layer (build the gridded transition estimates from 16GB of ragged, unevenly-sampled data), a statistical layer (the transition estimates are unreliable where drifters are sparse - this needs real inferential work, not just descriptive stats), and an interpretive layer (what the resulting connectivity pattern means and to whom).
It has a genuine, non-trivial statistics problem baked in: drifters cluster where the flow converges and thin out where it diverges, so raw cell-by-cell transition counts are noisy and biased toward well-sampled regions. Smoothing/regularising this defensibly is exactly what MATH3871 (Bayesian Inference and Computation) and MATH3821 (Statistical Modelling and Computation) are for.
It gives the business student a bounded, well-defined analytical seat (translating connectivity + uncertainty into a real-world risk/economic narrative) rather than an open-ended one.
Not locked into this - the lecture slides are explicit that projects are "guidelines towards a minimally viable product" and  may evolve from the starting point. But it's a strong fit for stream mix, so this plan is built around it.
Build in one extension from the start: the GDP overview document notes that "predicting where a drifter goes next" (starting project 3.2.4) differs from "where things end up" only in scale - a single drifter's near-term path versus the whole field's transport pattern. Transition matrix implicitly makes point predictions, so comparing those against the persistence and mean-flow baselines described in 3.2.4 is a coherent, low-cost add-on: it gives a second, independent way to demonstrate main product actually works, rather than asking the reader to take the transition matrix on faith.

All are data-rich course examples but Benguela + extended South Atlantic box was chosen for the stronger significance case and connectivity story.

4. Task ownership by stream
Computational stream (2 people)
Build the data access + wrangling pipeline: lazily loading the Zarr store via clouddrift/xarray, since the full dataset (~16GB, 197M rows) won't fit in memory - this is squarely a COMP9313 (Big Data Management) problem (chunked/out-of-core processing).
Implement the spatial gridding and transition-matrix construction (binning drifter positions into cells, counting transitions between cells over chosen time lags) - COMP3121 (Algorithm Design) for making this efficient at scale, COMP2521/COMP2041 for a clean, testable, version-controlled codebase.
Represent the transition matrix as a directed graph where useful (which cells are reachable from which, absorbing "coastline" states) - a natural application of MATH1081 (Discrete Mathematics) graph theory, and a nice bridge to COMP9417 (Machine Learning) if  want a supplementary predictive baseline later.
Own COMP3311 (Database Systems) aspects of structuring intermediate outputs so the quantitative stream can query them without re-running the whole pipeline.
Quantitative stream (2 people, including the double-major who can flex into computational work)
Own the core statistical problem: raw cell-to-cell transition counts are unreliable wherever drifters are sparse, and sparsity itself is not random (drifters gather where flow converges). This needs regularisation, not a raw frequency table.
MATH3871 (Bayesian Inference and Computation): put a prior over transition probabilities and borrow strength across neighbouring cells (hierarchical/empirical Bayes shrinkage). Don't stop at applying it - quantify how much it changes the picture: report how far the naive (unregularised) transition estimates drift from the regularised ones in the sparsest cells, and run a sensitivity check on how much the choice of prior strength affects the conclusions. That comparison is what turns "we used a Bayesian method" into a demonstrated result.
MATH2801 (Theory of Statistics): formal treatment of the estimators and their properties.
MATH3821 (Statistical Modelling and Computation): bootstrap/resampling-based uncertainty quantification as a complement or check on the Bayesian approach.
MATH2501 (Linear Algebra): eigen-decompose the transition matrix for its stationary distribution and mixing time - where does material end up in the long run, are there near-absorbing "sink" cells, how fast does the system forget its starting point. This is a strong, visual result for the poster, not just a theoretical aside.
MATH2871 (Data Management for Statistical Analysis): the statistical-side data wrangling once the computational stream hands off gridded output.
Business stream (1 person - scope deliberately bounded to one clear seat)
Own the "why this matters" case for the region (feeds the README and report background), grounded in literature as the past-student document recommends.
Own one clearly scoped analytical contribution: apply ECON2209 (Business Forecasting) techniques to the seasonal structure of the transition estimates - do connectivity patterns shift predictably by season? Treat this as a real forecasting exercise with a train/test split (e.g. forecast one year's seasonal pattern having only seen prior years), not a descriptive seasonal chart. Then quantify what a validated seasonal risk window is actually worth to a real decision-maker (e.g. spill-response planning, fisheries larvae dispersal, shipping risk) - a specific, defensible framing beats a general one. This is a bounded, well-defined task appropriate for one person's capacity, not an open brief.
Lead the translation of the modelling output into the non-technical narrative for the report's executive summary, poster and presentation - the past-student document is explicit that this needs to be written "for aliens," and that's a genuinely valuable, proportionate role for one person rather than asking them to match five people's technical output.
ECON2206/ECON3208/ECON3210 (introductory/applied/big-data econometrics) can inform how the forecasting and validation are framed and diagnosed, without duplicating the quantitative stream's inferential work.
Single-point-of-failure mitigation: the business stream owns the literature/significance case, the ECON2209 forecasting analysis, and the non-technical narrative - three deliverables on one person. Since the forecasting analysis is largely quantitative/computational in nature, the quantitative and computational streams will provide backup support on that specific task (train/test split design, sanity-checking the forecast against the transition-matrix output) to soften the load, without taking ownership of it away from the business stream.

6. Working norms
Shape the repo early and have every member commit in Week 1 (the lecturer needs to be invited as a collaborator).
Document delegated tasks and review weekly - the past-student advice and the course's individual-contribution assessment both point the same direction: an untracked division of labour is a liability, not just an inefficiency.
No changes to the original provided data - all preprocessing should be code applied to the raw data, so the pipeline is reproducible.
Nothing in the analysis should depend on which region R  picked - hand the code a different box and it should still run. Build this in from the start rather than retrofitting it before the final report.
Log AI use as  go, not reconstructed at report time - the final report requires a critical reflection on AI's role in  product, and a running log (what was asked, what was checked/changed) is far more credible and easier to write from than a memory-based reconstruction in the exam period.
Prepare for real domain questions at the poster session - Shane Elipot himself attends and will ask questions alongside staff. Whoever presents each component should be able to explain and defend the method to someone who knows this dataset intimately, not just present the result.
No pre-committed fallback method for the transition-matrix/regularisation work - if it doesn't converge or produces unexpected results as they emerge (e.g. by Week 6-7), we'll deal with it at that point rather than forcing a fix now: document what was tried, why it didn't work, and report it as a defensible negative result per the course's own framing, rather than hiding it.
